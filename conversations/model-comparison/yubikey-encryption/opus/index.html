This is a great, well-scoped setup and most of your plan is sound — but there are a couple of important architectural points (and one common misconception about how the Yubikey actually gets used on Android) that are worth nailing down before you commit. Let me verify the fiddliest current details rather than rely on memory, then give you a concrete plan.

Good — all three of the tricky points check out. Here's my advice, organized around the decisions that actually matter for your setup.

1. Reframe: you're moving from a symmetric container to GPG public-key encryption

Veracrypt is a symmetric volume: one passphrase-derived key, mount/unmount. The OpenKeychain + Yubikey world is asymmetric OpenPGP: you encrypt files to your public key, and the private encryption subkey (held on the Yubikey) decrypts them. This is the right primitive for your requirements and it's what makes "the key lives on a hardware token" possible at all. Concretely:

  • The Yubikey's OpenPGP applet has three slots: Signature, Encryption, Authentication. For decrypting files you only need the Encryption subkey on the card.
  • gpg --decrypt asks the card to unwrap the file's session key on-chip; the private key never leaves the token.
  • The public key is all you need to encrypt. It's not secret — put it on every device.

The main behavioral change vs. Veracrypt: there's no mounted volume by default. You decrypt files on demand (to stdout, a pipe, or tmpfs). If you miss the "mount a folder" feeling, see §5.

2. The off-token / multi-Yubikey requirement drives your whole key ceremony

This is the most important constraint you stated, and it dictates that you generate keys offline and keep an exportable backup — not generate them on the Yubikey. Two facts to internalize:

  • keytocard is destructive: moving the encryption subkey onto a Yubikey replaces the on-disk copy with a stub. You cannot pull it back off.
  • Therefore, to provision the same encryption subkey onto Yubikey #2, #3, … you restore your backup keyring between each keytocard.

Recommended ceremony (do it on an airgapped/offline boot or a throwaway GNUPGHOME):

  1. Generate a certify-only master key (kept offline) plus S/E/A subkeys. Use ed25519/cv25519 if all your Yubikeys are v5 (they support ECC OpenPGP and so does OpenKeychain); use rsa4096 if any are older NEO/4 tokens or you want maximum interop.
  2. Export and store the full secret keyring securely — this backup is your "use it bare" copy and your multi-token source: gpg --armor --export-secret-keys KEYID > master-secret.asc gpg --armor --export-secret-subkeys KEYID > subkeys.asc gpg --armor --export KEYID > public.asc gpg --gen-revoke KEYID > revoke.asc Keep these on encrypted offline media (and consider paperkey for the master). Put a strong passphrase on them.
  3. For each Yubikey: work from a fresh copy of the backup (e.g. export GNUPGHOME=$(mktemp -d); gpg --import subkeys.asc), then gpg --edit-key KEYID → key <n> (select the encryption subkey) → keytocard → Encryption slot → save. Discard that temp GNUPGHOME. Repeat per token.

Security tradeoff to accept consciously: keeping the encryption key exportable defeats the strongest property of a hardware token (non-extractability). Anyone who gets your backup + its passphrase can decrypt everything, forever, no Yubikey needed. That's a legitimate choice for your "provision many / use bare" goal — just guard that backup like it is the plaintext, because effectively it is. If you ever want the strong guarantee for a subset of use, that's when you'd generate on-card instead (but then it's single-token and non-portable).

3. Android reality — correct one misconception

Your mental model is right except for one thing: Termux does not talk to the Yubikey directly. Non-root Android apps can't grab a USB CCID smartcard, and NFC is only available to the foreground app. So the Yubikey is always driven by OpenKeychain (NFC tap or USB-C), and Termux delegates to it.

The bridge is okc-agent / okc-gpg (also packaged as okc-age): a shim that routes GnuPG operations from your Termux shell to OpenKeychain. Your ssh-client-in-Termux flow becomes, e.g.:

okc-gpg -d ~/vault/id_ed25519.gpg > "$TMPDIR/id_ed25519"   # NFC-tap the Yubikey when prompted
ssh -i "$TMPDIR/id_ed25519" host
shred -u "$TMPDIR/id_ed25519"

On Android put $TMPDIR on internal storage (tmpfs-ish, app-private), not the SD card, so plaintext isn't written to removable media. The encrypted .gpg files can live on the SD card as you planned. Import only your public key into OpenKeychain; the private E-key comes from the Yubikey.

4. WSL2 — two workable paths, pick the relay

There are two established approaches (both confirmed current):

  • A. gpg-agent socket relay (recommended). Install Gpg4win on Windows; Windows' gpg-agent owns the Yubikey. Bridge its socket into WSL2 with npiperelay + socat (or wsl2-ssh-pageant). WSL's gpg talks to the Windows agent; the Yubikey stays usable by Windows apps simultaneously. Cleaner, survives reboots, no custom kernel. See the WSL2 gpg-agent relay gist and Jarda Živný's guide.
  • B. usbipd-win passthrough. usbipd attach the Yubikey into the WSL2 VM and run pcscd/gpg inside Linux. Downside: it's exclusive — Windows loses the Yubikey while attached, and you can get stuck needing a reboot if Windows' gpg-agent grabbed it first. (Full FIDO2 needs a custom kernel, but for OpenPGP smartcard/CCID the stock kernel is fine.) See usbipd-win.

Go with A unless you have a reason to isolate the token inside the VM. It gives you the same decrypt-on-demand commands as Android, and as a bonus you get Yubikey-backed SSH auth (enable-ssh-support in gpg-agent) if you ever want it.

5. How to organize the files themselves

Three options, roughly best-to-worst for your case:

  • pass (passwordstore.org) — strongly recommended for the passwords, and fine for keys. It is your architecture, productized: a tree of per-file GPG-encrypted secrets, decrypted via your Yubikey. Mature cross-platform story: pass on desktop/Termux, the Android Password Store app integrates directly with OpenKeychain + Yubikey, and it syncs via git (or drop the store on your SD card / Syncthing). SSH private keys can be stored as multiline entries and decrypted on demand exactly like above.
  • Per-file gpg -e -r KEYID file for a handful of standalone files. Simplest; no extra tooling; decrypt to a pipe/tmpfs. Good if you don't want the pass layout.
  • "Poor-man's container" if you really want the mount feel: keep a gocryptfs directory whose password is stored in a small Yubikey-decrypted .gpg file. You okc-gpg -d passfile.gpg | gocryptfs ... to mount on Linux/WSL (FUSE) and via DroidFS on Android. This most closely reproduces Veracrypt (a real mounted encrypted folder) while still gating unlock behind the Yubikey. More moving parts — only worth it if the mount workflow matters to you.

My suggestion: pass for passwords and small secrets; per-file GPG for the occasional SSH key, both decrypting through the same Yubikey. It's the least custom, works identically on all three platforms, and matches the OpenKeychain/Termux tooling you already found.

Ok thanks that's interesting. I have used pass in the past in a different scenario but never quite understood its purpose in addition to bare GPG.

Right — and your instinct is correct: pass adds zero cryptography over bare GPG. Every entry is literally just a gpg -e file. If you find ~/.password-store, you'll see a plain directory tree of *.gpg files, and pass show foo is essentially gpg -d ~/.password-store/foo.gpg. So the fair question is "then why not just use gpg and a folder?" The answer is that pass is a set of conventions and ergonomics wrapped around exactly that. What you're buying:

1. A naming/layout convention. A predictable tree (web/github.com, ssh/hetzner, email/fastmail) with tab-completion on entry names. Bare GPG leaves you to invent and remember filenames and paths. Sounds trivial; it's most of the day-to-day value.

2. Convenience verbs over the gpg round-trip. pass insert, pass edit (decrypts to a tmpfile, opens $EDITOR, re-encrypts, cleans up), pass generate (makes a random password and stores it in one step), pass rm, pass mv/cp (which re-encrypt correctly). Each is a small gpg + tempfile + shred dance you'd otherwise script yourself and get subtly wrong (e.g. leaving plaintext in /tmp).

3. Clipboard hygiene. pass -c foo copies the secret to the clipboard and auto-clears it after 45s. That's the single most-used feature and it's annoying to reproduce by hand.

4. Structured-entry convention. pass treats the first line as the password and everything below as free-form metadata (username, URL, TOTP seed, recovery codes). Tools rely on this: pass otp (TOTP), browser/mobile integrations, passff for Firefox. Bare GPG files have no such contract, so nothing can introspect them.

5. Multi-recipient by directory. A .gpg-id file per subtree lists which key(s) encrypt that subtree, and pass init/pass git re-encrypt everything to the right recipients when membership changes. This is what makes team/multi-key stores tractable — with bare GPG you'd manually track "which files were encrypted to which keys" and re-run gpg -e across the tree on every change.

6. Git integration. pass git treats the store as a git repo with sane commits per change. Since ciphertext is what's committed, you get versioned, syncable, auditable history "for free." This is the backbone of the sync story (git remote, or Syncthing, or your SD card).

7. The ecosystem. Because everyone agreed on this trivial layout, you get the Android Password Store app, okc-gpg-based mobile use, browser extensions, rofi/dmenu pickers, etc. — all interoperating on the same folder. That network effect is the real payoff, and it's why it's worth preferring over a bespoke folder-of-gpg-files even though you could trivially build the latter.

So the honest framing: pass is a UX and convention layer, not a security layer. The security is 100% your GPG key (and thus, in your setup, your Yubikey). If you only have a handful of files and don't care about clipboard/TOTP/multi-recipient/git, bare gpg -e/gpg -d is genuinely fine and pass buys you little. Its value climbs with (a) the number of secrets, (b) how often you read/rotate them interactively, and (c) whether you want the mobile app and git sync — which, for your Android + multi-device goal, you probably do.

One relevant wrinkle for your specific case: pass shines for passwords and short secrets, but it's a bit awkward for whole SSH private key files (multi-line blobs where "first line is the password" doesn't apply, and you often want the key as a file on disk, not on a clipboard). For those I'd still lean on plain gpg -d key.gpg > tmpfile rather than forcing them into the pass model — which is exactly the "pass for passwords, per-file GPG for key files" split I suggested. Same Yubikey underneath either way.

Ok makes sense. Is there a way to pipe an ssh private key directly from gpg or pass into ssh-agent?

Yes — and it's the clean way to do it, because the plaintext key never touches disk; it lives only in the agent's memory. Modern ssh-add reads a key from stdin with the - argument:

# per-file GPG
gpg -d ~/keys/id_ed25519.gpg | ssh-add -

# from pass (entry content is the raw private key)
pass show ssh/hetzner | ssh-add -

# on Android/Termux, same thing through the Yubikey bridge
okc-gpg -d ~/vault/id_ed25519.gpg | ssh-add -

That's the whole trick. A few things worth knowing so it actually behaves:

Store the SSH key without its own passphrase. This is the important one. If the decrypted key still has an OpenSSH passphrase on it, ssh-add - can't prompt on the terminal — stdin is busy being the key — so it falls back to SSH_ASKPASS, and with no askpass/DISPLAY set it just fails. Since your GPG key (→ Yubikey) is already the protection layer, double-encrypting adds friction for no real gain. Generate the SSH key with an empty passphrase, then gpg -e it. If you do want both layers, set SSH_ASKPASS=/path/to/askpass and SSH_ASKPASS_REQUIRE=force so the passphrase can be collected out-of-band.

Add a lifetime so it self-expires:

gpg -d ~/keys/id_ed25519.gpg | ssh-add -t 3600 -    # forget after 1h

-c (confirm each use) also works but needs an askpass, so it's awkward in a headless Termux shell.

Make sure an agent is running and your shell points at it (echo $SSH_AUTH_SOCK). On Termux you may need to eval "$(ssh-agent -s)" in the session first; on WSL2 you'll typically already have one (or you're using the relayed Windows agent).

One nice wrinkle: this works even though the key came off a Yubikey-guarded file — the Yubikey unlocks the file, the resulting key sits in the agent, and ssh uses it normally with no further taps. If you find yourself doing this constantly for the same key, that's the point where it's worth considering skipping the file entirely and using the Yubikey's Authentication subkey as the SSH key via gpg-agent's ssh support (enable-ssh-support) — then there's no private key file to decrypt or pipe at all, and every SSH auth requires a physical touch. But for the "I have a few key files I occasionally load" case, the gpg -d | ssh-add - pipe is exactly right.

Ok but I've heard that there is a limit of only one Yubikey authentication subkey slot and I have to support at least two and possibly more legacy ssh rsa keys.

You've put your finger on exactly the limitation of the "use the Yubikey's own subkey as your SSH key" approach, and it's a real one — but it only applies to that approach. The good news is it doesn't affect the setup we just built.

The limit is real but narrow. The Yubikey's OpenPGP applet has exactly three key slots — one Signature, one Encryption, one Authentication. So if you go the gpg-agent enable-ssh-support route (Yubikey's Authentication subkey is your SSH identity, touch-to-auth), you get exactly one SSH identity. Full stop. That's the constraint you heard about, and for two-plus distinct RSA keys it's a dealbreaker.

But the gpg -d | ssh-add - model has no such limit. That's the key insight: in that model the Yubikey slot is not your SSH key — it's only the Encryption subkey that decrypts files. Your legacy RSA keys stay as N separate gpg-encrypted files:

gpg -d ~/keys/legacy-rsa-a.gpg | ssh-add -
gpg -d ~/keys/legacy-rsa-b.gpg | ssh-add -
gpg -d ~/keys/legacy-rsa-c.gpg | ssh-add -
# ...as many as you like

One Encryption subkey on the Yubikey unwraps all of them — there's no per-SSH-key slot involved, so "two, possibly more" is a non-issue. This also keeps working identically on Android (okc-gpg -d … | ssh-add -), which matters because the alternatives below mostly don't. This is the approach I'd recommend for you, precisely because it decouples "number of SSH identities" (unlimited, they're just files) from "Yubikey slots" (one, used only for decryption).

If instead you specifically want the keys non-extractable and living on the token, then you're looking at the Yubikey's other applet, PIV — which is where the multi-slot capacity lives:

  • PIV has the four named slots (9a/9c/9d/9e) plus ~20 "retired key management" slots (82–95), all usable for SSH via PKCS#11 (ssh -I .../libykcs11.so, or PKCS11Provider in your ssh config). So realistically up to ~24 SSH keys on one token, each touch-guarded.
  • You can import your existing legacy RSA keys into PIV slots (yubico-piv-tool -a import-key), so you don't have to regenerate them — and since you keep the originals, you can import the same keys onto multiple Yubikeys.
  • The catch, and it's a big one for you: PIV-over-PKCS#11 SSH has essentially no story on Android/Termux. OpenKeychain drives the OpenPGP applet, not PIV-for-SSH. So going PIV would split your workflow — on-device keys on desktop, but you'd fall back to decrypt-a-file on the phone anyway.

So the decision comes down to what you're optimizing:

Priority Approach
Many SSH keys, same workflow on Android + desktop, keys portable/backed-up Encrypted files + ssh-add - (Yubikey = decryption only) ← recommended
SSH keys non-extractable, on-device, touch-required, desktop-centric PIV slots + PKCS#11 (no Android SSH support)
A single flagship key with per-use touch OpenPGP Auth subkey via gpg-agent (the one-slot approach — too limited here)

Given your Android requirement and the fact that these are legacy keys you already hold as extractable material, the file-based model is the natural fit and sidesteps the one-slot limit entirely. The single OpenPGP Encryption subkey on each Yubikey is all the hardware you need, no matter how many SSH keys you end up with.
